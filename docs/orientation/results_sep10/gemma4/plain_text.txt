The river rises in the hills to the north and flows south through farmland before reaching the sea. In spring the water is high and fast, and the old bridge in the town is sometimes closed. Most of the people who live along its banks work on farms or in the small factories near the harbour.

The town itself grew up around a market that was held every Thursday. Farmers brought grain, vegetables and livestock, and merchants from the coast brought salt, cloth and tools. Over time the market became a permanent square with shops on three sides and a church on the fourth. The square is still used for a market today, although most of the goods now arrive by truck rather than by cart.

The climate is mild for most of the year. Winters are wet but rarely very cold, and snow falls only a few times each decade. Summers are warm and dry, which suits the orchards on the southern slopes. Apples, pears and plums are the main crops, and in a good year the harvest is large enough to be sold in the city as well as locally.

A railway line was built along the valley in the nineteenth century. It carried coal and timber down to the port and brought visitors up from the city on summer weekends. The line closed to passengers many years ago, but the station building survives and now houses a small museum about the history of the valley. The museum is open on weekends and has a collection of old photographs, tools and maps.

Education in the valley is provided by two primary schools and one secondary school. Students who want to continue their studies usually travel to the city, which has a university and several colleges. Many of them return after finishing their studies, and some have started businesses in the town, including a bakery, a bicycle repair shop and a small publishing house that prints books about local history.

The hills above the town are covered with forest. Oak and beech grow on the lower slopes, and pine grows higher up. Walking paths lead through the woods to a lake where people swim in summer and skate in winter when the ice is thick enough. Deer, foxes and many kinds of birds live in the forest, and the area has been protected as a nature reserve for several decades.

Local government is run by a council that meets once a month in the town hall. The council is responsible for roads, parks, waste collection and the library. Elections are held every four years, and turnout is usually higher than the national average. Meetings are open to the public, and residents often attend to discuss plans for new housing or changes to the bus timetable.

In recent years the population has grown slowly as families move out of the city in search of lower house prices and a quieter life. This has put some pressure on schools and roads, but it has also brought new shops and cafes to the town centre. Most residents say they are happy with the changes, although some worry that the character of the town may be lost if it grows too quickly.
